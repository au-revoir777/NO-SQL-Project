\begin{abstract}
Fraud rings share devices, cards and network addresses and move money among
themselves. Rule-based graph detectors suppress busy shared attributes such as
office gateways and carrier NAT with a degree cap. We study a streaming
pipeline that instead keeps those links as pairwise evidence, detects
incrementally on the region each micro-batch touches, and scores candidate
rings with a calibrated model. On a deterministic simulator with ring-level
ground truth and five evasion strategies, preregistered isolation experiments
on fresh test seeds show what matters: within the same pipeline a degree cap
costs \RfCapD{} ring F1 (95\% CI \RfCapLo{} to \RfCapHi{}) and most hub-hiding
rings, a weight that lets one busy attribute link a pair on its own costs
\RfNdOneD{}, and, given that no busy attribute can suffice alone, the shape of
the degree discount makes no difference. Against tuned rules, Louvain, Leiden
and FRAUDAR-style peeling, each with a scorer trained on its own candidates and
at least the same tuning budget, the pipeline improves ring F1 by
\RfExrulesperscenarioDA{} to \RfExpeelingDA{}. A GraphSAGE classifier trained on
ring labels from the same generator reaches higher F1 (\RfExgnnunscoredF{}
against \RfExproposedF{}), mainly on drifting and noisy streams, while the
pipeline ranks rings better and finds more adaptive and decoy rings;
\RfHySentence{} Slow and identity-rotating rings are rarely found by any
method, and all results are synthetic.
\end{abstract}
